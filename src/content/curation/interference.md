      Jason Hendardy is a photographer and artist based in Seattle, WA. He is interested in how people use images, objects, and spaces to make sense of a world that does not come already explained. His work often uses the language of documentation while pushing against its authority, treating photographs as unstable objects that shape what can be known.

      His project This Is a Test takes its title from the Emergency Broadcast System, which interrupted American television from the 1960s through the 1990s. Its purpose was to warn the public in the event of a nuclear attack. What aired was a sustained tone, a test pattern, and a flat voice delivering procedural information. No emergency was ever named. The result was uncertainty, a sense that life in America could change at any moment. This Is a Test associates that feeling with his parents’ immigration from Indonesia and the uncertainty immigrants like them have felt, and still feel, in the United States. The project brings together photographs often manipulated on television screens and Hi8 video stills drawn from 35 years of his family’s recordings.

      Wyatt Angelo is a visual artist based in Brooklyn, New York. He holds an MFA from the University of Hartford and a bachelor’s degree from Columbia University.

      In his work, he uses the familiar language of commercial advertising to draw attention to what happens when life is lived uncritically and an idea of success that is not our own takes root. There are forces at play that seek, through carefully staged photographs, to shape our desires. These images don’t necessarily manipulate us with malice, but they do guide us — gently, constantly — toward a version of life that benefits someone else. The worlds conjured by these entities are no more real than the images that advertise them, and yet to many they are the acme of what life should be.

      Angelo’s aim — in this project and in image-making more broadly — is to challenge this manipulative program by mimicking its surface while trying to disrupt it. He shares this work in the hope that other image makers might recognize just how powerful their tools have become.

      “Without overstating our role as photographers, we now operate in a landscape where images don’t just depict reality — they design the realities people strive to live in.”
